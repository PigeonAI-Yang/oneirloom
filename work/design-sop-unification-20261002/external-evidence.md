# External evidence for graphic-design SOP consolidation

Collection date: 2026-10-02. This is a source survey for the Primary's interpretation, not a proposed SOP. Sources were selected from original organizational, publisher, or educator pages. Findings are paraphrased.

## Source findings

### 1. Design Council — The Double Diamond

- Source: [The Double Diamond](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/)
- Author/date: Design Council; publication date is not displayed on the page.
- Finding: The model separates learning about a challenge from defining it, then opens and narrows solution exploration. Its delivery description includes trying alternatives at small scale and improving or rejecting them. The Council also says early making/testing may happen during discovery and that new learning can send a team back to earlier work.
- Implication question: Which parts of an existing graphic-design workflow benefit from naming problem exploration, brief clarification, concept exploration, and refinement, while allowing feedback to reopen earlier questions?
- Limitation: This is a high-level innovation model, not a graphic-production checklist. It does not establish mandatory stage boundaries, tools, counts, or typography and export checks.

### 2. AIGA — *Design Business + Ethics*, “A Client’s Guide to Design”

- Source: [AIGA official PDF](https://www.aiga.org/sites/default/files/2021-03/Design-Business-and-Ethics.pdf)
- Author/date: AIGA is the publisher; individual authorship and publication date could not be confirmed from the web-accessible extract.
- Finding: The indexed excerpt describes a brief as a statement of purpose rather than instructions for how to design. It says the brief can align expectations, state prioritized objectives and audiences/context, and serve as a benchmark for reviewing concepts and execution.
- Implication question: Which project facts must be settled in the brief, and which visual decisions should remain open for exploration?
- Limitation: The official PDF appeared in search results, but the web reader returned an internal error when opening it. Findings are limited to the indexed excerpt; no further access attempt was made.

### 3. AIGA Design Teaching Resource — “Conference Identity”

- Source: [Conference Identity](https://teachingresource.aiga.org/project/conference-identity/)
- Author/date: Educator Chantal Fischzang, Rutgers University–Newark; posted 2020-01-20.
- Finding: This identity-system course project links objective, target audience, motivators, message, and deliverables in its creative brief. It also describes audience-interest research, gathering visual associations, and using typographic grids to organize information across different media.
- Implication question: Should a reference-gathering step ask what associations matter to the intended audience and how the content/context should shape visual language?
- Limitation: It is a semester-long identity-system assignment with several media, so its scope and phases do not automatically fit a one-off graphic.

### 4. AIGA Design Teaching Resource — “ASU/JMU: Multi-Page Layout Magazine Design Project”

- Source: [ASU/JMU: Multi-Page Layout Magazine Design Project](https://teachingresource.aiga.org/project/magazine-design-project/)
- Author/date: Educator Marcia Pearl, Maryland Institute College of Art; student work credited to Amber Hooks, Elisha Davis, and Kelsey Stoneham; posted 2024-05-12.
- Finding: The assignment asks students to write a brief describing publication type, audience, and aesthetic before creating thumbnail layouts. It then asks for a grid, consistency across pages, and use of color systems and visual hierarchy.
- Implication question: Where could low-cost thumbnail layouts help compare composition options before detailed execution?
- Limitation: The assignment concerns a multi-page magazine. It supports thumbnails as one useful exercise in that context, not a universal requirement or fixed number of sketches.

### 5. Ellen Lupton — *Thinking with Type*, third edition

- Source: [PA Press publisher page](https://papress.com/products/thinking-with-type-3-edition)
- Author/date: Ellen Lupton; third revised and expanded edition published 2024-03-12.
- Finding: The publisher’s description covers type selection, spacing, alignment, grids, layout, visual balance, grouping, readability, legibility, and accessibility. It presents typographic hierarchy and grid as systems for organizing and communicating content, with examples and exercises.
- Implication question: Which hierarchy, grid, and readability checks should be visible in the design review for the actual format and audience?
- Limitation: This is the publisher’s summary, not the full book text. It supports the subject areas but does not provide a complete sequence or universal settings.

### 6. Letterform Archive — “Thinking with Type Workshop”

- Source: [Thinking with Type Workshop](https://letterformarchive.org/events/view/thinking-with-type-workshop/)
- Author/date: Workshop led by Ellen Lupton; held 2017-07-15 to 2017-07-16.
- Finding: The workshop description begins with studying archival design examples, then develops a typographic mark and a palette and applies the identity system to varied content. Its stated practice includes combining type, building hierarchy, and building a grid.
- Implication question: What kinds of references help explain a typographic direction, and how can a chosen system be checked across the intended content?
- Limitation: This is a short workshop outline, not a record of all teaching steps or a claim that every graphic project needs an identity system.

### 7. Adobe Learn — “Designing effective graphics”

- Source: [Designing effective graphics](https://www.adobe.com/learn/express/web/graphic-design-process)
- Author/date: Adobe Learn tutorial with graphic designer and educator Mariah Althoff; dated 2024-02-26.
- Finding: The poster example starts from the graphic’s goal and intended audience, then considers imagery, type, color, hierarchy, and refinement. It connects glance-level legibility to type choices and describes finishing adjustments to spacing, alignment, and color.
- Implication question: How should a design be checked at the size and viewing context for which it is intended?
- Limitation: It is a product tutorial built around one event-poster example. Its suggested font count and palette are example guidance, not universal standards.

### 8. Adobe Learn — “What makes a great layout?”

- Source: [What makes a great layout?](https://www.adobe.com/learn/illustrator/web/layout-basics)
- Author/date: Adobe Learn; no individual author is listed; published 2026-06-11.
- Finding: The article connects layout clarity to whitespace, grouping related information, repetition, contrast, alignment to a grid, focal points, and hierarchy. It describes type size, weight, and color as cues for relative importance.
- Implication question: Which message should be seen first, which details belong together, and what grid or alignment relationships will make the layout easier to follow?
- Limitation: This is a concise Illustrator learning article. It gives broad principles rather than a complete accessibility, production, or evaluation standard.

## Bounded cross-source observations

- Audience, communication goal, and constraints appear before visual choices in the AIGA brief examples and Adobe poster tutorial. These sources support clarifying the design task; they do not prescribe one canonical brief form.
- Iteration is supported at a broad level by the Design Council model, while AIGA's magazine exercise gives a concrete example of making thumbnails before developed page layouts. Neither establishes a universal number of concepts, review rounds, or prototypes.
- Reference work is supported as research and inspiration by the AIGA identity assignment, the Design Council's call to seek inspiration, and the Letterform Archive workshop. The sources do not establish a mandatory reference-board format, size, or comparison rubric.
- Concrete layout and typography topics supported by these sources include hierarchy, grids/alignment, grouping, contrast, whitespace, spacing, legibility/readability, and consistency across content. The sources do not specify one layout system or numerical thresholds suitable for every output.
- These are mixed-scope sources: innovation framework, professional/client guidance, educational assignments, book description/workshop, and software tutorials. Their examples can inform questions for an SOP, but they do not jointly prove that every project should follow the same fixed sequence.
