# Choosing useful demonstrations

Use this reference when an outline is broad but its examples are shallow. Select the rows relevant to the article. A topic is developed through decisions, visual differences, usable wording, and observed results, not by reaching a word count.

## Portrait and photography lessons

| Topic | Useful distinctions | What the illustration must let the reader inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose and reference reading | Avatar, outfit display, room atmosphere; visible source details versus requested changes | How crop and emphasis change with purpose; which details actually appear in the reference |
| Framing | Face, upper body, full body; image aspect ratio versus subject size versus crop | Top and bottom image edges, head and feet, face size, required hands or props |
| Camera position | High, level, low; height versus distance; camera tilt versus head tilt | Floor, bed surface, ceiling, near/far proportions, chin and shoulders |
| Orientation | Torso, head, and eyes; profile versus partial turn; rear view versus looking back | Shoulder line, visible garment front/back, nose direction, gaze; image-left versus the person's left |
| Composition | Center or side; small environmental figure versus close portrait; foreground framing | Person's position and size, useful space, foreground obstruction, furniture overlap |
| Pose | Standing, stepping, seat edge, reclining; supporting leg and relaxed leg | Feet on the floor, seat contact, backrest contact, arm support, coherent limb placement |
| Expression | Gaze versus head rotation; neutral, slight smile, tooth smile, closed eyes | Eyelids, cheeks, mouth, expression intensity; appearance changes beyond the expression |
| Hands and objects | Resting, touching hair, gripping a handle, supporting a cup bottom, using an armrest | Palm direction, contact surface, finger visibility, wrist shape, necessary frame space |
| Setting | Room category versus specific furniture layout; left/right versus near/far | Spatial relations that keep the intended setting recognizable; actual occlusion and perspective |
| Clothing | Silhouette and length; neckline, straps, sleeves; front/side/back design; layering; drape; fabric | Hem relative to knees and ankles, exposed cuffs, seams and lacing, which layer covers which, folds when sitting |
| Lighting | Direction, hardness, brightness, color, and day/night | Bright side of face and garment, shadow edges, hair rim, room brightness, recognizable material colors |
| Focus | Recognizable room versus blurred background; person versus near object in focus | Which object's detail is sharp, degree of blur, whether both objects remain sharp despite the request |
| Full prompts | One coherent goal; replacing a section without contradictory leftover demands | Whether every requested visible feature fits the chosen crop, pose, setting, and light |
| Corrections | Missing detail, wrong relation, conflicting requirements, unintended extra changes | Actual before/after difference, what was changed in the prompt, remaining misses |
| Multi-panel images | Shared appearance and setting; per-panel action; expression grid versus pose grid | Panel count, crop, pose, face, clothing construction, hair, room details across panels |
| Practice | One main change with its necessary related edits; describing an unfamiliar image | Saved prompt and output, intended change, other changes, a concrete next revision |

Clothing is a common depth trap: front-view satin versus knit covers only surface appearance. When the lesson promises clothing control, choose examples across the garment's shape, construction, angle, layering, and behavior during movement. Apply the same breadth test to other chapters rather than expanding clothing alone.

Do not force portrait topics into unrelated tutorials. For illustration, useful choices may include line quality, shape, palette, edge treatment, and composition. For product or poster lessons, prioritize product geometry, lettering, spacing, material, and legibility. Show the distinctions the promised result actually needs.

## Turning a demonstration into a lesson

Use a local, concrete explanation rather than a report of operations. For example, a cup lesson can proceed as follows:

- Explain the difference between holding the side of a cup and supporting its bottom.
- Show an image where that contact can be seen, with enough space for both hands.
- Give a prompt excerpt naming the upward-facing palm under the cup and the other hand at the handle.
- If the actual first result held the cup's side, explain that visible miss before describing the revision. Do not imply this happened unless the run exists.
- Note unrelated visible changes only when they matter to interpreting the example.

For crop lessons, distinguish writing a tighter crop from actually obtaining one. For camera lessons, distinguish moving the camera from lowering the head. For grids, evaluate layout and appearance continuity separately. For focus lessons, inspect the subject that should be blurred, not just the background wall.

## Language revision without changing the evidence

Prefer concrete actions over abstract editorial labels. Write that a hand rests on a knee, a cardigan covers the waist, or the lower edge of the photo stops at the waist. Avoid describing these as an anchor, control dimension, acceptance gate, or information layer in learner-facing prose.

Keep necessary terms and explain them briefly. First person should sound like a teacher describing choices, with varied sentence lengths and ordinary transitions. Remove repeated declarations that an image is not a perfect experiment once the relevant limitation has been stated clearly beside the example.

Polish explanatory excerpts freely while preserving their intended visual meaning. Preserve exact historical prompts when presented as actual submitted text. If the revised prompt changes the task or observed feature, label it as a suggested revision or test it through the authorized tool before claiming a result.
